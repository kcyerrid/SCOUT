# Compromise Hardware Supply Chain (T1195.002)

## 1. Summary
Compromise Hardware Supply Chain describes adversary manipulation of physical devices or hardware components prior to delivery or deployment. By embedding malicious functionality at the hardware or firmware level, attackers can establish highly persistent and difficult-to-detect initial access.

This subtechnique is typically associated with advanced, well-resourced adversaries due to the complexity and cost of execution.

---

## 2. Technical Overview
Adversaries compromise hardware supply chains by:
- Modifying firmware or BIOS images before delivery
- Implanting malicious components during manufacturing or transit
- Abusing insecure hardware update mechanisms
- Tampering with peripherals or embedded devices

Artifacts may include unexpected firmware behavior, anomalous network traffic originating from devices, integrity check failures, or discrepancies in hardware inventories.

---

## 3. Subtechnique Considerations
T1195.002 focuses specifically on **physical hardware and firmware compromise**, distinguishing it from:
- Software update compromise
- Dependency or development tool compromise

Detection is challenging due to limited visibility into hardware internals and infrequent firmware inspection.

---

## 4. Procedure Examples
Observed adversary procedures include:
- Preloading malicious firmware on network devices
- Altering BIOS/UEFI images prior to system delivery
- Implanting covert communication capabilities in peripherals

Analysts may observe persistent anomalous behavior surviving OS reinstallation or disk replacement.

---

## 5. Detection Guidance
Detection should emphasize integrity and anomaly detection:
- Firmware integrity verification and attestation
- Unexpected network traffic from hardware devices
- Deviations from known-good hardware configurations
- Asset inventory mismatches or unexplained components

Hardware-level compromise often requires specialized detection tooling.

### Data Source Notes
- **Firmware Logs:** Limited availability but high value when present
- **Device Management Logs:** Useful for detecting configuration drift
- **Network Traffic:** May reveal covert communications

---

## 6. Response Guidance
When hardware supply chain compromise is suspected:
- Isolate affected devices immediately
- Remove hardware from production environments
- Reflash firmware from trusted sources where possible
- Coordinate with vendors and manufacturers for investigation

Preserve affected hardware and logs for specialized forensic analysis.

## 8. SOC Relevance
Hardware supply chain compromise poses an extreme risk due to stealth and persistence, but occurrence is relatively rare. SOC teams should treat credible indicators as high-severity events requiring immediate escalation.

---

## 9. Threat Actor Usage
This subtechnique is primarily associated with:
- Nation-state intelligence services
- Strategic espionage operations
- Highly targeted campaigns against critical infrastructure

Confidence in usage by advanced actors is high.

---

## 10. Campaign Usage
Compromise of hardware supply chains has appeared in:
- Strategic espionage campaigns
- Long-term access operations against high-value targets
- Critical infrastructure intrusion scenarios

---

## 11. Malware Usage
Malware associated with hardware supply chain compromise often includes:
- Firmware-level implants
- Bootkits and pre-OS payloads
- Covert command-and-control mechanisms embedded in devices

Traditional endpoint malware may not be present.

---

## 12. Mitigations
Effective mitigations include:
- Vendor security assurance and audits
- Hardware attestation and secure boot enforcement
- Firmware signing and validation
- Strict chain-of-custody controls
- Asset inventory verification

---

## 13. Testing & Validation
Validation approaches include:
- Firmware integrity checks and attestation testing
- Red team simulations of compromised hardware scenarios
- Vendor-led security assessments

Successful validation identifies unauthorized hardware or firmware modifications.
